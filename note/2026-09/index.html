Rebalancing Your Life: Why “Downsizing” Misses the Point

You’ve spent the last 15, 18, even 25 years focused on one thing: raising a family. And you’ve done a fantastic job of it.

That big house made sense then. It held the bedrooms, the space for holidays, the rooms that always seemed full. But those same rooms now sit quiet. If you’re honest, you probably haven’t been upstairs in months—except to grab something in storage or dust a few shelves. Meanwhile, the costs keep coming: higher electricity, higher water bills, roof repairs, ongoing maintenance. You’re still paying for square footage you no longer need. I don’t call this downsizing. I don’t even call it rightsizing. Those words feel incomplete. What you’re really doing is rebalancing.

Rebalancing means taking a clear look at where your energy and money are going and asking a simple question: Does this still serve the life I want next? The equity in that house is not just locked up. It’s sitting there waiting to work for your next chapter. Sell the house, put that equity to work in the market, and free yourself from the ongoing expenses of a home that no longer matches your season of life.

You’ve already poured years into raising a family. Now it’s time to pour that same intentional focus into making the years ahead the best ones yet.

That’s what rebalancing is all about.

One Thing Worth Knowing

The Fruit That Actually Built Pearland

Pearland gets its name from the pear trees that caught the eye of Polish nobleman Count Witold von Zychlinski when he platted the town in 1894 (originally filed as “Pear Land”). But the fruit that brought real prosperity wasn’t the pear. It was the fig.

Around the turn of the 20th century, a peddler sold what he claimed were magnolia trees in the area. When they flowered and fruited, locals discovered they were a large, juicy variety of fig that thrived in the local soil. The “Magnolia fig” quickly became a major cash crop across northern Brazoria County—especially around Pearland, Friendswood, and Alvin. Orchards produced thousands of pounds per acre, and processing plants sprang up (including one in Pearland that employed 75–100 people, mostly women). The industry was a multi-million-dollar business before the Depression and freezes took their toll.

By the mid-teens through the 1940s, figs—not pears—were the economic backbone that helped the community recover after the devastating 1900 and 1915 hurricanes nearly wiped out the early town. Next time you drive through Pearland or Manvel, remember: the name may come from pears, but the real local success story grew on fig trees.

Worth the Drive

A Free Ferry Ride and a Sugar Donut

More and more, families are having a hard time finding things to do together that don't cost a fortune. I get it. So let me give you the perfect one. Take the ferry from Galveston Island over to Bolivar Peninsula. It's completely free, and if you've got kids, they're going to love every minute of it. I know, because one of the best memories of my whole childhood is a free ferry ride and a sugar donut.

My family didn't have much money when I was a kid, and my mother wore that struggle right on her face. There were five of us kids, and like my mom always said, "kids aren't cheap." So we didn't spend money on big family outings. We just didn't have it to spend. The good thing was we grew up in the Pacific Northwest, where a lot of the best stuff was free. We'd take a drive down the scenic highway. We'd visit a state park. We'd spend the whole day out at Lost Lake. It wasn't fancy, but it got us out of the house, and it got us together.

One of the things I remember most from living near the Puget Sound was taking the ferry. I was maybe six or seven. When we drove our car right up onto the boat, I thought it was the coolest thing I'd ever seen. Then the seagulls came, wheeling around everywhere. I'd never seen anything like it.

But the thing I remember most is the sugar donuts. My mom loved sugar donuts. And we hardly ever got sweets, because sweets were an extra, and extras weren't in the budget. All of us kids understood that. So when my mom came around and handed each of us our own sugar donut, that was a big deal. A really big deal.

I can still see it. Standing on that ferry, looking out over the Puget Sound, eating my donut. And when I looked over at my mom, she wasn't eating. She was watching us, with the biggest smile on her face. She got more out of giving us that little bit of happiness than we ever got out of the donut. It's one of the fondest memories I have of her.

So here's what I want you to take from this. That day cost my mother nothing for the ferry and pocket change for the donuts. And all these years later, it's one of the memories I hold onto most. It was never about the money. It was about being together, and about someone finding real joy in making us happy.

So take the ferry. Get the donut. The memory's free.

Cheers. Phil

Cooking with Phil

Fettuccine Alfredo

Fettuccine Alfredo is so versatile. It can be a great starch to complement your entree or it can be the entree itself. By adding shrimp or chicken.

Ingredients - 1 lb fettuccine - 6 tablespoons butter - 1 garlic clove, minced - 1½ cups heavy cream - 1¼ cups shredded Parmesan cheese, divided - ¼ teaspoon salt - ¼ teaspoon black pepper - 2 tablespoons chopped Italian parsley (optional) - Reserved pasta water, as needed

**Instructions**

1. Bring a large pot of salted water to a boil. Add the fettuccine and cook according to package directions until al dente. Reserve about ½ cup of the pasta water, then drain the pasta.

2. While the pasta cooks, melt the butter in a large skillet over medium heat. Add the minced garlic and cook for 1–2 minutes, until fragrant (do not let it brown).

3. Pour in the heavy cream and bring to a gentle simmer. Cook for 5–8 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the cream reduces slightly and thickens.

4. Reduce the heat to low. Add half of the Parmesan cheese and whisk until the cheese is fully melted and the sauce is smooth. Season with salt and pepper.

5. Add the drained fettuccine to the skillet and toss to coat. Sprinkle in the remaining Parmesan and continue tossing until the pasta is evenly coated. If the sauce is too thick, add a splash of the reserved pasta water until it reaches your desired consistency.

6. Taste and adjust seasoning if needed. Garnish with chopped Italian parsley, if using, and serve immediately.
